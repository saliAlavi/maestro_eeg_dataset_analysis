\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and task.}
MAESTRO \citep{maestro2026} has sixteen listeners and one hundred trials each.
In every trial four talkers speak at once from loudspeakers at fixed positions,
and the listener is told beforehand which one to attend to. Trials last about
$30$\,s. Each talker recording is used only once, so no audio is shared between
training and test. The attended talker is about $15$\,dB louder than the others
in every trial. We use five streams, all resampled to $64$\,Hz and standardised
per channel: EEG ($32$ channels), gaze ($6$), head motion ($6$), optical flow
from the scene camera ($4$), and one new stream, a \emph{frozen} V-JEPA\,2 ViT-L
embedding \citep{assran2025vjepa2} of the part of the scene the listener is
looking at (one $1024$-d vector per window, never trained). Signals are cut into
windows of $W$ seconds with hop $H$ ($W\!=\!10$, $H\!=\!5$ unless stated). Each
example holds the streams, $K$ candidate speech envelopes of which one is
correct, the index of the correct one, the loudspeaker each candidate came from,
and the listener's identity. The candidates are placed in random order in every
window, so their position says nothing. At test time the order is fixed by the
window's index, so every model we compare sees exactly the same inputs
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

Every result is defined by four choices: the candidate set, the split, the
decision window and the set of streams. Every accuracy comes with its own
shuffled baseline; an accuracy without one is not a result. Besides accuracy,
the baseline and the contribution, we report a permutation $p$-value
$(\#\{v_{\text{null}}\!\ge\!v\}+1)/(n+1)$, whose smallest possible value with
twenty shuffles is $0.048$; the Shapley credit $\phi_m$; the \emph{flip rate},
the fraction of windows whose chosen candidate changes when the stream is
shuffled; accuracy with the stream replaced by zeros; and the \emph{collapse},
the mean cosine similarity between the time-centred embeddings of different
windows, where a value near one means every window produces the same pattern.

Training uses AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with learning rate $10^{-3}$,
batches of $32$ windows from one listener, and at most $50$ epochs. Checkpoints
are chosen on validation data by the rule of Section~\ref{sec:selection}, with
three shuffles, and the chosen checkpoint is scored once on the test set with
twenty. \textbf{We did no hyper-parameter search}: every setting was fixed once
in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:hp}), so these numbers are not tuned. To compare
architectures, the first row of our ablation runs the benchmark's model
\citep{maestro2026} --- its encoder, its cosine-then-linear score and plain
cross-entropy --- inside our training loop and splits, so that differences
between rows come from the model and not from the protocol. We run it on both
candidate sets. Neither run is meant to reproduce the benchmark's published
numbers, which use its own training loop, a longer schedule and all streams;
when we cite those numbers we take them from the paper.

\paragraph{Why this dataset.}
Crowding out between streams can only be studied when the streams are recorded
\emph{and} the listener is free to produce them; Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews why the other public corpora rule this out by design. As far as we know,
MAESTRO is the only public AAD dataset that records eye tracking, head motion
and scene video together with EEG from a listener who is free to move in a room
with several loudspeakers (Appendix~\ref{app:corpora}). The parts of \merit{}
that do not need these streams --- the audio probe and the coupling score ---
work on any matching dataset, and we test them on two
(Section~\ref{sec:res-mm}).
