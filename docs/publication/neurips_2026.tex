\begin{abstract}
Selective auditory attention is a fundamentally multimodal phenomenon: when attending to a speaker in a noisy environment, listeners orient their gaze, adjust their head position, and use visual cues - such as lip movements and speaker location - to support comprehension. Yet Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD) - the task of inferring the attended speaker from brain signals for applications such as neuro-steered hearing aids or prostheses - has been studied almost exclusively using electroencephalogram (EEG). This leaves open questions about how behavioral signals, such as gaze, head motion, and scene video, contribute to decoding, and whether fusing these modalities with EEG can yield more robust decoders. We introduce MAESTRO, the Multimodal Auditory-attention Ego-centric Speech-TRacking Open corpus, the first AAD dataset to simultaneously record EEG, eye gaze, pupillometry, head-mounted scene video, and head inertial measurement unit (IMU) data from 16 participants across 100 trials. Furthermore, unlike prior datasets, MAESTRO uses four competing English speakers with dedicated background noise sources and systematically varies the attended speaker’s signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) between 0 and 18~dB, enabling the study of attention decoding across a realistic range of acoustic conditions. Alongside the data, we release an evaluation protocol comprising four tasks, four data splits, six decision windows, and three baseline models. Our benchmarks demonstrate the value of treating attention as a multimodal phenomenon and open the door to future applications, additional analyses, and further methodological advances.

\end{abstract}



\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The cocktail party problem describes the human ability to selectively attend to a single speaker in noisy, multi-talker environments \cite{cherry1953}. In practice, this ability is not purely auditory: listeners naturally orient their gaze toward the attended speaker, adjust their head position to improve spatial hearing, and use visual cues such as lip movements and speaker location to disambiguate competing voices. These coordinated auditory, visual, and motor behaviors are an integral part of how humans solve the cocktail party problem in the real world. 

Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD) formalizes this problem computationally, seeking to infer the attended speaker from measurable brain signals, with the long-term goal of developing hearing prostheses and smart hearing aids that can steer amplification toward whichever speaker the listener is focusing on~\cite{geirnaert2021}. 
%
The primary modality for AAD has been electroencephalography (EEG), which captures the brain's electrical responses to auditory stimuli with millisecond precision and can be worn comfortably in naturalistic settings due to its noninvasive nature, high temporal resolution, and portability~\cite{geirnaert2021}. Yet by recording only EEG, existing datasets implicitly treat selective auditory attention as a purely neural phenomenon, overlooking the gaze shifts, head re-orientations, and visual context that are an integral part of how listeners attend to a speaker in the real world~\cite{geirnaert2021, rotaru2024, golumbic2013, hendrikse2019, geirnaert2024}.

To address this gap, we introduce the Multimodal Auditory-attention Ego-centric Speech-TRacking Open (MAESTRO) corpus, a 16-subject, 100-trial dataset that simultaneously records five synchronized data streams per trial: 32-channel EEG, binocular gaze, pupillometry, head-mounted scene video, and head inertial measurement unit (IMU) data. Each trial consists of four competing English speakers sourced from LibriSpeech~\cite{librispeech2015} and two background noise sources from CHiME-Home~\cite{chime2015}, with the attended speaker’s signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) varied between 0 and 18~dB across 100 trials per participant - features absent from all prior AAD corpora (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). We treat MAESTRO not merely as a collection of recordings, but as the foundation for an evaluation protocol: a defined set of four tasks, four evaluation splits, six decision windows, and three baselines that should be reported for AAD experiments. The four splits are designed to answer distinct questions about decoder performance that matter for real-world deployment: how well a decoder works within a subject given subject-specific training data, how well it generalizes to new subjects without calibration - a critical requirement for practical hearing prostheses, how much of their signal reflects genuine neural activity once behavioral confounds are removed - essential for any claim of purely neural decoding, and how it performs on the highest-quality data - providing an optimistic upper bound that motivates future data collection efforts. Alongside the protocol, we provide reproducible baselines spanning envelope reconstruction, source attention detection, source-location attention detection using spectral EEG features, gaze, IMU, scene video, and multimodal fusion as inputs. We further conduct diagnostic analyses that assess whether EEG-based decoders capture genuine neural activity or behavioral confounds, and whether successful decoding reflects speech comprehension or listening effort.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section \ref{sec:relatedwork} describes the related EEG datasets that motivated our work. The data-collection study procedures are detailed in Section \ref{sec:dataset}. The experimental setup is discussed in Section \ref{sec:experiments} and benchmarks for using the data with subsequent baseline results are provided in Section \ref{sec:benchmarks}. Additional details, analyses, and results are provided in the Appendix.

\section{Related Work: AAD Corpora}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

Existing AAD datasets have been instrumental in advancing the field. The KUL~\cite{biesmans2016} and DTU~\cite{fuglsang2017} corpora established the two-speaker envelope reconstruction paradigm that remains the field's primary benchmark. The NJU~\cite{nju2023} dataset explored how speaker location affects decoding by presenting the same two-speaker scenario across multiple spatial configurations, and the ASA dataset~\cite{lin2024} extended this further by introducing 10 competing speaking locations spanning angles from $-90°$ to $+90°$. The ESAA~\cite{esaa2022} dataset expanded coverage to Mandarin-speaking populations, broadening the demographic reach of AAD
research, while the MM-AAD dataset~\cite{mmaad2025} took a step further by pairing EEG with audiovisual stimuli, demonstrating that visual information can improve decoding accuracy. 

\textbf{Limited acoustic environments.} Together these datasets have enabled reproducible method comparisons and underpinned a decade of steady progress in decoder development.  Yet they share design constraints that limit what questions can be asked of them. Most present speech dichotically with no background noise, draw stimuli from non-English languages, and do not vary the SNR systematically across trials (see Table \ref{tab:comparison}). These choices matter: restricting stimuli to two speakers with no background noise produces conditions that are far simpler than real listening environments, limiting the ecological validity of trained decoders. Drawing stimuli exclusively from non-English languages restricts applicability to the large English-speaking populations and to models trained on English speech corpora. And without systematic SNR variation, it is impossible to study how acoustic difficulty affects decoding performance or to build decoders that are robust to varying levels of noise - a critical requirement for real-world hearing prosthesis applications where background noise levels change continuously.

\textbf{Limited modalities.} In addition, all existing datasets record EEG in isolation, ignoring the physiological and behavioral signals that naturally accompany auditory attention ~\cite{geirnaert2021, rotaru2024, golumbic2013, hendrikse2019, geirnaert2024}. Rotaru et al.~\cite{rotaru2024} showed that many spatial AAD approaches - that is, attempts to decode whether the attended speaker is to the left or
right of the listener - may be confounded by involuntary gaze shifts
toward the attended speaker, suggesting that EEG-only datasets overestimate
purely neural decoding performance. The MM-AAD dataset~\cite{mmaad2025} demonstrated that visual information improves decoding by pairing EEG with video recordings of the attended
speaker's face, yet captures neither natural gaze behavior nor head movements. 
Since gaze, head orientation, and visual cues are
directly correlated with auditory spatial attention, recording these signals
alongside EEG would enable richer multimodal decoders and provide a
baseline against which purely neural decoding claims can be
evaluated.

\section{The MAESTRO Dataset} \label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Study Design}

Participants were seated in a centrally-located chair within a quiet,
controlled room and surrounded by six loudspeakers as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:setup}. Four speech speakers $S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$, and $S_4$
were placed in front of the participant at angles of $-67.5^{\circ}$,
$-22.5^{\circ}$, $22.5^{\circ}$, and $67.5^{\circ}$ with respect to the
frontal axis, spanning both hemispheres and two levels of eccentricity. This
four-speaker layout allows attention to be decoded at multiple levels of
spatial granularity: whether the attended speaker is on the left or right,
whether they are near or far from the centre, and which of the four speakers
is being attended to specifically. This is a richer spatial design than the
binary left/right paradigm used in all prior AAD datasets. Two additional
loudspeakers $N_1$ and $N_2$ were placed behind the participant at
$-135^{\circ}$ and $135^{\circ}$ respectively, delivering background noise
to increase the acoustic complexity of the scene and improve ecological
validity. All loudspeakers were placed 4~ft from the participant.

The experiment had two main phases: Training phase, and Main phase. There were a total of $105$ \textbf{"trials"} in the experiment where the first five trials are the \textbf{Training phase} and the rest of the $100$ trials are part of the \textbf{Main phase} for the study. During each trial, speech and noise stimuli were played individually and in combination through these loudspeakers to examine how varying configurations of speech and background noise influence auditory attention. A mono-color marker was placed on top of each loudspeaker to provide a visual cue corresponding to each audio source. Participants were instructed to attend to a pre-designated speech signal among the four speakers in the front ($S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$, and $S_4$) and subsequently answer multiple-choice questions about it. These comprehension questions served to verify that participants attended to the correct audio source. Participant responses were recorded using custom software loaded on a laptop. Throughout the experiment, participants wore multiple sensors to simultaneously capture neural and physical cues associated with auditory attention. Eye gaze, first-person video, and head movements were recorded using \textbf{Tobii} glasses. Neural responses were recorded using an \textbf{ANTNeuro} EEG headset containing 32 wet electrodes non-invasively placed on the scalp. A conductive gel was applied between each electrode and the scalp to ensure appropriate signal conductivity.


Both Training and Main phases are identical, but the Training phase is intended to familiarise participants with the experimental procedures, sound stimuli, and user interface. Participants were also given the opportunity during this phase to ask questions about the study procedures.


The Main phase was divided into two sessions of $25$ minutes each, and each session contained a total of $50$ trials. There was a mandatory $10$-minute break between the two sessions. However, participants were encouraged to take breaks as needed to minimise fatigue and potential response bias.

At the beginning of the experiment, all participants provided demographic information and disclosed any audio, visual, mental, or physical conditions that could interfere with task performance. Participants with any such conditions were excluded from the study. All collected demographic information was anonymized and stored privately and securely.

\subsection{Participant Recruitment and Screening}
A total of 16 participants took part in this study, with a mean age of 23.4 years (range: 18-30 years), comprising 9 women and 7 men (14 right-handed, 2 left-handed). All participants were required to be at least 18 years of age, native English speakers, and free from any audio, visual, mental, or physical conditions that could interfere with task performance. Participants were recruited through electronic and paper communications and compensated with monetary incentives. The study was approved by the
Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to data collection, and all
participants provided written informed consent before taking part.

Prior to participation, all recruits underwent two pre-screening tests to
ensure that cognitive and auditory function did not confound task
performance. A cognitive screening via the Mini-Mental State Examination
(MMSE)~\cite{folstein1975mini} was administered, requiring a score of 25 or
above, to ensure participants had sufficient cognitive capacity to follow
instructions and sustain attention across the full session. An audiological
screening via ReSound's online hearing test~\cite{resound_hearing_test} was
conducted to verify that hearing thresholds were within a range compatible
with the speech stimuli levels used in the experiment, permitting up to
moderate symmetric sensorineural hearing loss (pure-tone averages across
0.5, 1, and 2~kHz between 15 and 55~dB~HL). Participants with severe
hearing loss were excluded as this could impede audibility of the speech
stimuli even after amplification, potentially compromising the validity of
attentional judgments. Pre-screening data were not retained after eligibility determination. Full inclusion criteria and equipment accommodations are detailed in Appendix \ref{app:participants}.

\subsection{Audio Stimuli and Comprehension Questions}

% Speech stimuli were sourced from the \textbf{LibriSpeech} corpus 
% \cite{panayotov2015librispeech} and background noise from the \textbf{CHiME-Home} 
% dataset \cite{foster2015chime}. Each trial consisted of four unique 30-second 
% speech signals played through the front loudspeakers and two unique 30-second

Speech stimuli were sourced from the \textbf{LibriSpeech}
corpus~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech}, a large-scale collection of
read English audiobook recordings, chosen for its wide speaker diversity,
high recording quality, and open license. Background noise was sourced from
the \textbf{CHiME-Home} dataset~\cite{foster2015chime}, a collection of
domestic soundscape recordings capturing realistic everyday noise, chosen
to simulate the kind of diffuse background noise encountered in real
listening environments. Each trial consisted of four unique 30-second
speech signals played through the front loudspeakers and two unique
30-second noise signals played through the rear loudspeakers, yielding
630 unique audio files and 420 unique speakers across 105 trials. All
audio files and speakers were non-repeating across trials, ensuring that
participants could not rely on familiarity with previously heard material. The attended speaker's SNR was varied between 0 and 18~dB and distributed normally across trials (mean 
$\approx$~12~dB), a systematic acoustic difficulty manipulation absent from 
all prior AAD datasets. SNR was controlled by adjusting the per-channel playback power of each loudspeaker
on a trial-by-trial basis. Specifically, for each trial the power of the
attended speaker's channel was set relative to the combined power of all
other speech and noise channels to achieve the target SNR. The full SNR distribution is provided in 
Appendix~\ref{app:stimuli}.

Following each trial, participants answered a multiple-choice comprehension 
question about the attended speaker to verify attentional compliance. Questions 
were generated using the GPT-4o API from stimulus transcripts, manually 
inspected for consistency, and included a dedicated ``I could not pay 
attention'' option to flag trials with complete attentional failure. Full 
generation details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:stimuli}.

Behavioral performance is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}. Mean 
comprehension accuracy across all 16 participants was 83.2\%, with all but 
one subject (Subject~1, 63\%) falling between 74\% and 92\%. A consistent 
first-to-second session improvement was observed across almost all participants 
(80\% to 87\% group mean), suggesting a familiarization effect driven by 
the mandatory 10-minute inter-session break. Subject~1 showed the most 
pronounced improvement (58\% to 69\%) while Subject~2 showed no change 
(86\% in both halves). Per-trial correctness labels are released alongside 
the dataset to allow downstream work to filter by compliance.

\subsection{Recorded Multimodal Sensory Data Streams}

During the experiment, EEG, gaze, scene video, and IMU data were recorded 
simultaneously across all trials. EEG was acquired using the ANTNeuro EEG 
headset, while gaze, video, and IMU data were captured using Tobii Pro 
Glasses~3. Custom synchronization software ensured temporal alignment across 
all modalities. Recording specifications for each modality are summarized in 
Appendix~\ref{app:recording}.

\subsubsection{EEG}

EEG was recorded continuously at 500~Hz across 32 electrodes placed on the
scalp in a standard 10--20 montage using the ANTNeuro \emph{eego mylab}
amplifier. Electrode placement followed the international 10--20 system
(Fp1, Fpz, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6, M1, T7, C3, Cz,
C4, T8, M2, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, POz, O1, Oz, O2),
with a linked-mastoid online reference and an ADC clip at 0.0839~V.
Conductive gel was applied between each electrode and the scalp to minimize
contact impedance and ensure signal quality throughout the session. The
hardware sample counter channel was used to verify acquisition integrity;
timestamp intervals were perfectly uniform at 2.0~ms with no detected gaps
or dropped samples across any subject or trial.

For preprocessing, raw signals should be re-referenced to a common average
and bandpass filtered at 1--45~Hz to remove DC drift and high-frequency
noise. Mastoid electrodes M1 and M2 should be excluded from the common average
reference due to their susceptibility to saturation artefacts; channels
flagged as bad were spherical-spline interpolated prior to re-referencing,
ensuring every trial contributes 32 valid channels to downstream analysis.
Full bad-channel detection criteria and per-subject channel quality metrics
are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:quality}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{isc_topography.png}
    \caption{Mean ISC topography computed using the leave-one-out method

Inter-Subject Correlation (ISC)~\cite{hasson2004, kuruvilla_mathew2021},
which measures the consistency of neural responses across participants to
the same stimulus and serves as a data-driven index of stimulus-driven
neural engagement, was computed using a leave-one-out method across all
100 trials. Peak synchrony was observed in left fronto-temporal regions,
particularly at FC5 (Fig.~\ref{fig:isc_topo}), consistent with the
well-established role of the left hemisphere in speech and language
processing~\cite{ocklenburg2018, hagoort2005} and with prior work showing
that ISC reflects selective attentional engagement to speech in
competing-speaker paradigms~\cite{kuruvilla_mathew2021}. Band power
topography across delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma bands is provided
in Appendix~\ref{app:recording}.


\subsubsection{Gaze}
Eye-tracking data were collected at approximately 50~Hz using the Tobii
Pro Glasses~3, recording three-dimensional gaze direction vectors and pupil
diameter for each eye independently, as well as a two-dimensional
scene-projected gaze coordinate that maps the gaze point onto the
participant's field of view. This provides a continuous record of where the
participant was looking at every moment during each trial. Total gaze recording time across
all participants and trials was approximately 15.1~hours. Data quality was
high across all participants, with a mean tracking loss of only 1.54\%. Pupil diameter was bilaterally symmetric and within the
physiologically normal range (left: $3.29 \pm 0.62$~mm; right: $3.28 \pm
0.57$~mm). Detailed gaze quality and dispersion characteristics are
provided in Appendix~\ref{app:recording}.

\subsubsection{Video}
Scene video was recorded continuously throughout each trial using the 
wide-angle scene camera mounted on the Tobii glasses, capturing the 
participant's first-person field of view as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fpv}. 
The scene video provides a continuous visual record of the participant's 
viewing environment, enabling extraction of optical flow features for 
downstream multimodal analysis. Full encoding specifications are provided 
in Appendix~\ref{app:recording}.

\subsubsection{IMU}
Head movement data were recorded at 120.6~Hz using the three-axis 
accelerometer and gyroscope embedded in the Tobii glasses. The mean 
gyroscope RMS was 6.95~deg/s across all subjects and trials, with 
Subject~13 showing markedly elevated movement at approximately 12~deg/s, 
exceeding 1.5 times the group mean. Movement was highest at trial onset 
and stabilised from Bin~3 onward, mirroring the gaze dispersion pattern, 
suggesting an initial head adjustment followed by sustained stillness 
during active listening. Per-subject and within-trial movement profiles 
are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:recording}.

















% \section{Introduction}

% Auditory attention decoding (AAD) is the problem of inferring, from non-invasive recordings of a listener, which of several concurrent talkers they are attending to. Public datasets have driven progress by releasing EEG plus the attended and unattended audio: KUL~AAD~\cite{das2019aad}, DTU~AAD~\cite{fuglsang2018zenodo}, and recent MEG-AAD releases cover most published decoders. These datasets capture speech-tracking EEG but \emph{not} the behavioral channels that generate the recurring reviewer concern that spatial AAD is entangled with overt orientation: pupil dilation follows arousal, gaze trajectories lateralise toward the attended source~\cite{golumbic2013}, and subtle head orientation tracks effortful listening~\cite{hendrikse2019}. Without synchronous gaze and head-motion streams it is not possible to ask, on the same trials that support the AAD claim, how much of the classifier is neural and how much is overt behaviour.

% \paragraph{What this dataset adds.} For every trial we have (i)~32-ch \SI{500}{\Hz} EEG; (ii)~binocular gaze direction, pupillometry, and scene-projected gaze; (iii)~\SI{100}{\Hz} head-mounted IMU; (iv)~\SI{25}{\Hz} egocentric scene video; (v)~the stereo audio played through each of three speaker devices; and (vi)~a comprehension question answered after every trial. No previously released AAD corpus bundles all five non-audio streams.

% \paragraph{What we do with it.} We treat the corpus not as a bag of recordings but as the substrate for a \emph{contract}: the set of tasks, splits, windows, and baselines a paper must report on if it claims an AAD result on this data. Around that contract we ship reproducible baselines on three task framings and a strict motion-residualisation control that gives a confound-controlled floor against which any ``purely neural'' decoder must compete. We use the multimodality to settle---quantitatively, on the same trials---two long-running questions: \emph{is the EEG decoder eye-tracking in disguise?} and \emph{do AAD decoders track comprehension or effort?}

\section{Experimental Setup and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Benchmarking Tasks} \label{ssec:tasks}

MAESTRO defines three classification tasks and one continuous 
envelope-reconstruction track. To ensure that results reported across 
independent studies remain directly comparable, any paper reporting AAD results on MAESTRO must report at least one task, but preferably all four.

\textbf{T1 (attended hemisphere, chance 0.5)} asks whether the attended 
speaker is on the left ($\{S_1, S_2\}$, at or below $-22.5^\circ$) or 
right ($\{S_3, S_4\}$, at or above $+22.5^\circ$). This is the same binary 
framing used in most existing AAD datasets, making it the primary basis for 
comparison with prior work.

\textbf{T2 (attended eccentricity, chance 0.5)} asks whether the attended 
speaker is in the inner ($\{S_2, S_3\}$, $\pm 22.5^\circ$) or outer 
($\{S_1, S_4\}$, $\pm 67.5^\circ$) position. This task checks whether a 
decoder is responding to how far the speaker is from the centre rather than 
which side they are on. Comparing T1 and T2 performance helps identify what 
spatial information a decoder is actually using.

\textbf{T3 (four-class attended source, chance 0.25)} asks which of the four 
speakers is being attended to. This is the hardest task and the most 
informative one, as it requires the decoder to distinguish between speakers 
on the basis of identity rather than just side or distance.

\textbf{T4 (envelope reconstruction)} is a continuous decoding track. 
Performance is measured using the difference in Pearson correlation between 
the reconstructed EEG response and the attended versus unattended speech 
envelopes ($\rho_{\text{att}} - \rho_{\text{una}}$), and as binary attendance 
accuracy at decision windows of $\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 30\}$~seconds. This track 
is included because envelope reconstruction is the most widely adopted 
evaluation approach in prior AAD work, and reporting it allows direct 
comparison with the existing literature. Information Transfer Rate (ITR) is also 
reported at every window to support benchmarking of real-time methods.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Splits} \label{ssec:splits}

MAESTRO should be split defines four evaluation splits. Together they are designed to give 
a complete picture of decoder performance: how well it works within a subject, 
how well it generalizes to new subjects, how much of its signal is genuinely 
neural, and how it performs on the highest-quality data.

\textbf{S1 (within-subject five-fold stratified cross-validation, seed 0)} 
is the primary split. The model is trained and tested on data from the same 
subject, using five-fold cross-validation. The data is split into training and evaluation by a ratio of 80:20. Accuracy is reported as the 
unweighted mean across all 16 subjects, with 95\% confidence intervals 
estimated by resampling subjects 10{,}000 times. This split measures the upper bound of decoder performance under
ideal conditions, where subject-specific training data is available, and
serves as the primary basis for comparing decoding methods on this dataset.

\textbf{S2 (leave-one-subject-out)} tests how well the model generalizes to a completely new subject it has never seen during training, making it
the more realistic scenario for real-world deployment in hearing prostheses
where subject-specific calibration data may not be available. The gap
between S1 and S2 quantifies the subject-specific calibration premium:
how much decoder performance depends on having seen data from that
individual, which is a critical consideration for designing practical
AAD systems.

\textbf{S3 (motion-residualized within-subject)} is designed to control for 
a specific confound: the fact that when a listener attends to a speaker, they 
also tend to orient their head and eyes toward that speaker. This means 
EEG-based decoders may be picking up motion-related signals rather than 
purely neural ones. In this split, the contribution of gaze, IMU, and video 
signals is removed from the EEG features before classification. So, this is basically S1 but without gaze, IMU, and video data. A decoder 
that claims to be purely neural must show improvement on this split 
specifically.

\textbf{S4 (high-quality tier within-subject)} restricts evaluation to 
subjects who met two pre-registered quality criteria: a comprehension accuracy 
of at least 0.80 and a Tobii gaze validity of at least 0.70. At protocol 
version 1.0, 8 of 16 subjects meet both criteria. This split gives an 
optimistic upper bound on what is achievable with clean data, while keeping 
the selection criteria fixed and transparent so that it can be reproduced 
exactly by future work.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:protocol}

\subsubsection{Decision Windows and Metrics}
For classification tasks T1, T2, and T3, the canonical decision window is 
the full 30-second trial. For the envelope reconstruction track T4, accuracy 
must additionally be reported at decision windows of $\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 30\}$ 
seconds, along with the corresponding information transfer rate at each window. 
The primary evaluation metric is accuracy reported with bootstrap 95\% 
confidence intervals. Per-subject accuracy scatter and information transfer 
rate are reported as secondary metrics.

\subsubsection{Recommended Baselines}
Any paper reporting results on MAESTRO must reproduce and report the following 
three baselines, each of which is reproducible. All three use linear or low-capacity classifiers intentionally: logistic regression and ridge regression are well-understood, have no tunable architecture hyperparameters, and produce results that are
fully reproducible across hardware and software environments. This makes them reliable reference points that future work can compare against without ambiguity. Linear models also establish a meaningful performance floor - any deep learning decoder that cannot substantially outperform a logistic regression on this data has not demonstrated a genuine capacity advantage. We note that deep EEG decoders, including convolutional and transformer-based
architectures are natural next steps for this dataset and are expected to improve on these baselines; their absence from this release reflects the scope of a dataset paper rather than a limitation of the data itself. Deep model results will be added in a future analysis pack update without modifying any dataset files.

\textbf{B1 (gaze-only logistic regression, S1, T1)} achieves a hemisphere 
accuracy of 0.770. This is the minimum threshold any new EEG-based decoder 
must exceed before claiming a meaningful multimodal contribution, since gaze 
alone already provides a strong attentional signal in this paradigm.

\textbf{B2 (368-dimensional EEG spectral feature logistic regression, S1, 
T1)} achieves a hemisphere accuracy of 0.716. This represents the performance 
ceiling of non-deep EEG-based methods on this dataset and is the appropriate 
comparison point for future deep EEG decoders.

\textbf{B3 (EEG spectral classifier on motion-residualised features, S3, T1)} 
achieves a hemisphere accuracy of 0.521. This is the confound-controlled floor. 
Any gain over B3 represents decoding performance that cannot be attributed to 
motion-related signals, and is therefore the most meaningful result for a paper 
claiming purely neural decoding gains.

Multimodal methods must additionally report a \textbf{marginal gain number}, defined 
as S1 accuracy minus the better of gaze-only (B1) and EEG-spectral-only (B2). 
This requirement exists because pooled fusion accuracy can obscure whether a 
gain comes from genuine fusion or from one dominant modality. As shown in 
Section~\ref{sec:fusion}, pooled fusion can hide substantial per-subject 
variation, and this requirement ensures that future papers surface that effect 
explicitly.

\section{Benchmarks and Results}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

We report baseline decoding performance across four modalities following the
protocol in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. The goal is to characterise what each
modality contributes individually and in combination, and to define the
performance floors and ceilings that future work should be measured against.
All baselines are reproducible with a single command via the released
evaluation harness.

\subsection{Envelope Reconstruction Baselines}
\label{sec:trf}

delta/theta, 28-band mel filterbank, and CCA on the same filterbank), each

\subsection{Classical Spectral EEG Classifier}
\label{sec:spectral}

\subsection{Unimodal Behavioral Baselines: Gaze, IMU, and Scene Video}

\subsection{Multimodal Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

We evaluate four fusion strategies combining EEG spectral features and gaze:
early fusion (LightGBM on concatenated features), mean late fusion, calibrated
late fusion (posteriors weighted by inner-CV accuracy above chance), and
stacked fusion (second-stage logistic regression on concatenated posteriors).
Pooled across subjects, no strategy exceeds the gaze-only baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:fusion}). Per-subject, fusion provides meaningful gains when
both modalities are individually strong: Subject~7 improves from 0.89 to
0.97, Subject~15 from 0.92 to 1.00, and Subject~2 from 0.91 to 0.95.
Calibrated late fusion never drops below either unimodal baseline, as its
weights automatically collapse toward the stronger modality. This divergence
between pooled and per-subject results is why the evaluation protocol requires
multimodal methods to report marginal gain numbers alongside pooled accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), and why per-subject scatter is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:bench}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

At 16 participants, MAESTRO is sufficient for within-subject AAD analysis and
comparable to the KUL corpus, but LOSO results should be interpreted as a
calibration reference rather than a reliable estimate of deployment
performance. The stereo-device design means streams on the same side share
nearly identical temporal envelopes, so classical envelope-based decoders
cannot be ported without modification; this is intentional but should be
noted by users. Subject~1, who had hardware failures on both mastoid
electrodes, is included in all pooled results and excluded only under the
pre-registered S4 split. Demographic coverage is incomplete as age and gender
were collected voluntarily, and only aggregate statistics are released. The
shared variance between EEG spectral features and head/gaze orientation
reflects a genuine underlying entanglement rather than a simple artefact;
downstream work claiming purely neural gains should report performance on S3
alongside S1. Several of these limitations, including cohort size, demographic
coverage, and the absence of deep model baselines, will be addressed in future
dataset and analysis pack releases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The dominant narrative in AAD research has been that EEG is the signal of 
interest, envelope reconstruction is the evaluation method, and improving 
decoder accuracy is the goal. MAESTRO challenges all three assumptions 
simultaneously.

We release MAESTRO, a multimodal AAD dataset with five synchronised data 
streams from 16 participants across 1,600 trials, alongside a versioned 
evaluation protocol with four tasks, four splits, six decision windows, and 
three mandatory baselines. The benchmarks produce four findings only possible 
with a multimodal release: envelope reconstruction sits at chance while a 
spectral EEG classifier exceeds it by 21.8 percentage points; gaze alone 
outperforms both EEG decoders pooled, yet per-subject fusion never 
underperforms the stronger modality; motion residualisation collapses the 
spectral classifier to chance, defining a confound-controlled floor for future 
purely neural decoders; and gaze-based decoding tracks comprehension while 
envelope-tracking anti-tracks it, dissociating acoustic attention from 
comprehension.

We hope MAESTRO shifts the conversation from how to improve EEG decoding 
accuracy toward what is actually being decoded and how we can know.

\subsection{Participant Inclusion Criteria and Equipment Accommodations}
\label{app:participants}

\paragraph{Cognitive screening.} Participants were required to show no signs of dementia, verified through the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)~\cite{folstein1975mini}. The MMSE is a brief screening tool (approximately 3--5 minutes) that evaluates cognitive function across orientation, memory, attention, language, and visuospatial ability, with a maximum score of 30 points. A score of 25 or above was required for inclusion; scores below 24 may indicate cognitive decline such as dementia or memory-related conditions.

\paragraph{Audiological screening.} Participants were permitted to have up to moderate symmetric sensorineural hearing loss, confirmed via ReSound's online hearing test~\cite{resound_hearing_test} (approximately 3 minutes), which assesses hearing using device speakers or headphones. Pure-tone averages (PTA) across 0.5, 1, and 2~kHz were required to fall between 15 and 55~dB~HL. Participants with hearing loss exceeding this threshold were excluded, as severe hearing loss could impede audibility of speech stimuli even after amplification, potentially compromising the validity of auditory judgments.

\paragraph{EEG gel allergy screening.} As the ANTNeuro EEG headset employs gel-based sensors applied to the scalp, all participants were asked for known allergies to the conductive gel contents prior to participation.

\paragraph{Vision correction accommodation.} Participants who wear prescription glasses were required to remove them and wear the Tobii Pro Glasses 3 instead, which are equipped with 44 interchangeable lens inserts ranging from $-8.0$ to $+3.0$ diopters in intervals of 0.5 diopters for both eyes, accommodating the most common forms of near- and farsightedness. Participants were therefore required to provide their eye prescription prior to participation.

normal distribution centred at approximately 12~dB, spanning 0 to 18~dB 
across the 100 evaluation trials, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}. This 
systematic variation was designed to assess how acoustic difficulty affects

\subsection{Recording Specifications and Hardware}
\label{app:recording}

\paragraph{EEG.} The ANT Neuro \emph{eego mylab} amplifier recorded 34
channels at 500~Hz in a standard 10--20 montage (Fp1, Fpz, Fp2, F7, F3,
Fz, F4, F8, FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6, M1, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, M2, CP5, CP1,

\subsection{Feature Descriptions}
\label{app:features}

\paragraph{Envelope reconstruction backbones.}
Four backbone variants are evaluated: a broadband Hilbert envelope filtered 
between 1 and 9~Hz, a split delta (1--4~Hz) and theta (4--8~Hz) band 
envelope, a 28-band mel filterbank envelope, and a CCA model applied to the 
same mel filterbank. For all variants, a ridge regression backward TRF is 
fitted over EEG lags between $-200$ and $+500$~ms against the attended speech 
envelope, with the regularisation parameter selected by inner five-fold 
cross-validation over $10^{-1}$ to $10^{6}$, scored by 
$\rho_{\text{att}} - \rho_{\text{una}}$.

\paragraph{EEG spectral features (368 dimensions).}
Log Welch band powers in delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma per channel 
(160); within-channel band ratios (96); region-wise alpha lateralisation 
indices (4); Hjorth mobility and complexity per channel (64); Shannon spectral 
entropy per channel (32); region-wise $1/f$ aperiodic slope (4); alpha-band 
magnitude-squared coherence and weighted phase-lag index within-left, 
within-right, and between-hemisphere (6); global field power mean and standard 
deviation (2).

\paragraph{Gaze features (23 dimensions).}
Scene-projected mean and standard deviation of gaze position, per-eye azimuth 
and elevation, pupil diameter, sample validity, saccade rate and median 
amplitude, three-dimensional gaze centroid, and head rotation magnitude.

\paragraph{IMU features (35 dimensions).}
Per-axis mean, standard deviation, and range of accelerometer and gyroscope 
signals, dominant motion frequencies via FFT, and spectral band ratios of the 
gyroscope signal across low, mid, and high frequency bands.

\paragraph{Scene video features (15 dimensions).}
Mean and standard deviation of frame-level motion energy, mean brightness, 
and dense optical flow statistics including mean magnitude, standard deviation, 
and directional components, computed on a $160 \times 90$ pixel downsample.



\subsection{Data Storage, Layout, and Alignment}
\label{app:storage}

\paragraph{Directory structure.} Per-subject data are split across two
parallel directory trees partitioned by modality:

\begin{verbatim}
experiment_data/
    Subject_N/
        Eval-1/ ... Eval-105/
            eeg_data.p
            eeg_time_data.p
            audio_timestamps.json

Video Recordings/
    Subject_N/
        <trial_folder>/
            gazedata.gz
            imudata.gz
            scenevideo.mp4
            events.json

audio_stimuli_data/
    pairs/*.flac
    trials.csv
\end{verbatim}

Folders 1--5 are training trials; folders 6--105 are main trials. Subjects
1--2 use literal numeric folder names (\texttt{1}\ldots\texttt{105}).
Subjects 3--16 use ISO-8601 timestamps (\texttt{YYYYMMDDTHHMMSSZ}); the
loader parses the embedded time and sorts ascending to recover the natural
recording order, verified empirically against audio playback timestamps to
$r_\Delta \ge 0.99$ trial-to-trial for every subject. The file
\texttt{audio\_stimuli\_data/trials.csv} lists the attended-speaker index,
device files, SNR, and per-channel powers for every trial.

\paragraph{Cross-modal temporal alignment.} All streams are clipped to the
shared audio-playback window $[t_0, t_1]$ recovered from each trial's
\texttt{audio\_timestamps.json}. The EEG internal clock is mapped to
wall-clock time via the \texttt{first\_sample\_time} field in
\texttt{eeg\_time\_data.p}. Tobii recording-relative timestamps are shifted
to align their first sample to $t_0$. Audio envelopes are computed from each
device's stimulus file and resampled to the target analysis rate (commonly
64~Hz to match the EEG low-pass). An alignment sanity check is provided in
\texttt{analysis/01\_data\_audit.ipynb}.


\subsection{Data Quality}
\label{app:quality}

\paragraph{EEG bad-channel detection and referencing.} A channel is flagged
bad if (i)~its standard deviation is below $10^{-9}$\,V (flat), (ii)~at
least 10\% of samples reach the 0.0839\,V ADC clip (saturation), or
(iii)~its AC variance has $|z|>6$ relative to the median-absolute-deviation
of the remaining channels. The dominant failure mode is mastoid saturation:
Subject~1 has 100\% flat M1 and 100\% saturated M2, and M2 saturation is
common cohort-wide. Referencing falls back to common-average over good
channels when either mastoid is bad; flagged channels are then
spherical-spline interpolated. Every trial therefore has 32 valid channels
entering downstream analyses regardless of raw electrode issues.
Per-subject and per-channel bad-rates are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:eeg_quality}.

\subsubsection{Generalization Summary}
\label{app:sweeps}

The four generalization stress tests below are required entries for the
evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); full tables are in
Sections~\ref{app:snr}--\ref{app:loso}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SNR stratification.} Envelope-CCA hemisphere accuracy rises
monotonically from 0.464 at $\le 6$\,dB to 0.525 at $\ge 15$\,dB
(+6.1\,pp). The spectral classifier peaks in the middle bins (0.670 at
7--10\,dB), weakest at low SNR (poor neural SNR despite high listening
effort) and at high SNR (where the task becomes trivial and listeners may
lapse). Gaze accuracy is nearly flat across SNR, consistent with gaze
indexing the spatial-attention decision rather than acoustic difficulty.

\item \textbf{Window sweep.} Envelope CCA stays at chance for $\le 4$\,s
and reaches 0.539 at 30\,s; the spectral classifier is defined only at the
30-s trial window. Continuous-AAD methods must report all six windows.

\item \textbf{Learning curves.} Gaze-only saturates by $n_\text{train}{=}40$;
the spectral classifier is still gaining between $n{=}60$ and $n{=}80$,
implying additional accuracy is recoverable from longer sessions. Pooled
early fusion stays below gaze-only at every $n$.

\item \textbf{LOSO (S2).} Envelope CCA transfers at 0.539; gaze 23-D at
0.715; the spectral classifier at 0.615 (drops 10.1\,pp vs.\ S1,
quantifying the per-subject calibration premium); concatenated EEG$+$gaze
at 0.696. Gaze dominates cross-subject because between-subject EEG
topography variation is much larger than between-subject gaze-behaviour
variation.
\end{itemize}

Every cleaning step retains $\ge 0.67$ hemisphere AAD; channel-wise
gaze$+$IMU regression costs only $-0.7$\,pp ($0.716 \to 0.709$); the
largest drop ($-3.9$\,pp) comes from removing 9 EOG-/EMG-prone channels
entirely. The remaining 23 central/parietal/occipital channels are exactly
where auditory cortex projects (Cz, Pz, CP1/2, C3/4, P3/4, POz, O1/Oz/O2).
The signal is not in the EOG-prone channels and does not vanish under
standard nuisance removal at the time-series level: the simplest version of
``is this just eye-tracking?'' is answered in the negative.

EEG spectral content is strongly linearly related to head-motion amplitude,

motion direction, and the AAD signal is not a per-trial scalar audio summary.

\subsubsection{Trial-level Motion-residualisation: A Confound-controlled Floor}
\label{app:resid}

Per CV fold, we fit $M = \text{Ridge}(\alpha{=}10)$ on $Z_\text{train} \to
X_\text{train}$ where $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 71}$ is the concatenated
gaze (23-D) $+$ IMU (35-D) $+$ video (13-D) summary and $X \in
\mathbb{R}^{n \times 368}$ is the EEG spectral feature matrix; we replace
$X$ with residuals $\tilde{X} = X - M(Z)$ and refit the AAD classifier. We
compare to a shuffle-motion null in which $Z$ is row-permuted across
trials, measuring the capacity cost of orthogonalisation per se.

\centering
\small
\caption{Motion-residualisation results. Wilcoxon $p$ paired against raw,
$n{=}16$. The lower CI of motion-residualised T1 is below chance.}
\label{tab:resid}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Task & raw & shuffle-motion & motion-residualised \\
\midrule
T1 hemisphere  & \textbf{0.716} [0.641, 0.793] & 0.643 [0.585, 0.701] ($p{=}10^{-3}$)      & \textbf{0.521} [0.488, 0.552] ($p{=}6{\cdot}10^{-4}$) \\
T2 inner-outer & 0.650 [0.592, 0.714]          & 0.610 [0.559, 0.665] ($p{=}0.018$)         & 0.510 [0.480, 0.536] ($p{=}10^{-3}$) \\
T3 4-class     & 0.442 [0.357, 0.535]          & 0.392 [0.328, 0.462] ($p{=}0.017$)         & 0.270 [0.239, 0.296] ($p{=}4{\cdot}10^{-3}$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Orthogonalising with a 71-D shuffle costs 7\,pp on T1; orthogonalising with
the real motion features costs a further 12\,pp. The residualised number
(0.521, lower CI 0.488) sits at the chance floor, confirming that a
substantial fraction of the discriminative information co-varies with head
and gaze orientation. This is neither an artefact discovery nor a refutation
of the AAD signal: attending to a lateralised speaker produces both
lateralised neural activity and lateralised head/gaze orientation as aspects
of the same attentional process. What this control delivers is a
confound-controlled floor (0.521, chance 0.5) that future work must move
when claiming purely neural gains, not the raw S1 number alone. A continuous
sweep $X_\alpha = X - \alpha M(Z)$ for $\alpha \in [0,1]$ shows a smooth,
monotone cost curve (Section~\ref{app:partial}), consistent with genuine
entanglement rather than a clean separable subspace.

\subsubsection{Behaviour $\times$ Decoder: Comprehension vs.\ Effort}
\label{app:behxdec}

For every decoder we computed $\Delta_s = P(\text{decoder correct} \mid
\text{behav.\ correct}) - P(\text{decoder correct} \mid \text{behav.\
wrong})$ per subject, then a signed Wilcoxon test across 16 subjects.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\caption{Within-subject behaviour-$\times$-decoder paired test. $\Delta_s$
is the per-subject difference in decoder accuracy between correctly and
incorrectly answered comprehension trials.}
\label{tab:behxdec}
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
Decoder & Task & Median $\Delta_s$ & Sign ($+/-$) & Wilcoxon $p$ \\
\midrule
Backward-TRF split $\delta{+}\theta$ & envelope-attendance & $-0.084$ & 3/13 & \textbf{0.034} \\
Backward-TRF broadband               & envelope-attendance & $-0.068$ & 5/11 & 0.093 \\
CCA mel-28 (gaze-residualised)       & envelope-attendance & $-0.087$ & 5/11 & 0.117 \\
Gaze 23-D                            & T1 hemisphere       & $+0.068$ & 11/5 & \textbf{0.044} \\
Spectral 368-D                       & T1 hemisphere       & $+0.037$ & 9/7  & 0.180 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two opposite-sign findings sit beside each other: gaze-based AAD is
positively correlated with comprehension correctness ($p{=}0.044$), while
envelope-tracking AAD is negatively correlated, reaching nominal $p{<}0.05$
on the split $\delta{+}\theta$ TRF. The 368-D spectral classifier sits in
between (median $\Delta_s{=}+0.037$, $p{=}0.18$) and dissociates from both
signs. Gaze-based decoders track comprehension-relevant orienting behaviour:
subjects who comprehend more fixate the attended speaker more consistently.
Envelope-tracking TRF decoders read out a cortico-speech alignment that the
listener can sustain without successfully recalling the question, and may
recruit more on trials where comprehension is hard, consistent with
effort/arousal accounts of low-frequency speech tracking. Two practical
consequences: a paper should not claim that a TRF-based decoder reflects
successful comprehension; and the dataset can be used to develop
listening-effort probes (split $\delta{+}\theta$ TRFs as a candidate) that
are statistically dissociable from comprehension-correlated gaze decoders.

\subsection{Ethics and broader impact}
\label{sec:ethics}

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (protocol number and institution disclosed in the camera-ready version). All participants gave written informed consent specifically covering the release of de-identified EEG, eye-tracking, IMU, and head-mounted scene video. Scene video is the only modality capable of re-identifying participants or bystanders: we release the head-mounted egocentric footage (no participant face), with faces of incidental persons in the lab blurred by an automated pipeline (MediaPipe face detector at threshold $0.3$ followed by manual review). Participants were compensated at the minimum-wage rate in the study's jurisdiction.

\paragraph{Intended use.} The corpus is released for research on multimodal attention decoding, hearing-aid algorithms, and brain--computer interfaces. Foreseeable misuses include using per-trial behavioral data to infer trait-level attributes of participants, and applying AAD decoders in surveillance contexts to infer listening targets; the CC-BY-NC-4.0 licence and access-by-request gate for the raw scene video are our mitigations.
